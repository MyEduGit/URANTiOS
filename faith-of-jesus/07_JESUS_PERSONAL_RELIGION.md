## Q1. What did Jesus himself believe about God?

**Urantia account.** Jesus "saw God as being holy, just, and great, as well as
being true, beautiful, and good. All these attributes of divinity he focused in
his mind as the 'will of the Father in heaven'" (196:0.2). His God was
simultaneously "The Holy One of Israel" and "The living and loving Father in
heaven" — he did not originate the Father-concept but "exalted and elevated the
idea into a sublime experience... proclaiming that every mortal creature is a
child of this Father of love" (196:0.2). His "great contribution... was not
that he revealed so many new ideas about the Father" but that he "demonstrated
a new and higher type of *living faith in God*" (196:0.3). He believed the
universe is fundamentally friendly and the Father's overcare certain: "he never
religiously doubted the certainty of God's watchcare and guidance" (196:0.1);
"a profound confidence in the universe — just such a trust as the child has in
its parental environment" (196:0.11). The content of his gospel followed: the
fatherhood of God and the sonship/brotherhood of man (98:7.11; 141:7.4;
195:10.21).

**Gospel parallels (TEXTUAL OBSERVATION).** Abba-address (Mark 14:36); the
Father who sees in secret (Matt 6); "no one is good but God" (Mark 10:18,
paralleled at 196:0.9 and 196:2.2); providence over sparrows and lilies
(Matt 6:26–30; Luke 12).

## Q2. How did Jesus trust God?

Not as consolation: "he did not resort to faith merely as a consolation in the
midst of difficulties" (196:0.3), but as "the tranquillity of supreme and
unquestioned trust" and "the tremendous thrill of living, by faith, in the very
presence of the heavenly Father" (196:0.3). The trust was **childlike but not
childish** (196:0.12), fear-free ("His faith grew to such heights of trust that
it was devoid of fear," 196:0.11), and **fanaticism-proof** — it "never became
fanatical, for it never attempted to run away with his well-balanced
intellectual judgments" (196:0.7). Under ultimate test it held: "this superb
faith was undaunted even by the cruel and crushing threat of an ignominious
death" (196:0.5). The trust was reciprocal toward people: "Jesus had so much
faith in them" (171:7.8) — trusting God entailed believing in men (196:1.5).

## Q3. How did Jesus pray and make decisions?

**Prayer.** The definitive Urantia statement is 196:0.10: he "never prayed as a
religious duty"; prayer was (among a twenty-two-fold enumeration) "a
declaration of soul loyalty... a transcendental surrender of will... the mighty
mobilization of the combined soul powers." "The secret of his unparalleled
religious life was this consciousness of the presence of God; and he attained
it by intelligent prayer and sincere worship — unbroken communion with God —
and not by leadings, voices, visions, or extraordinary religious practices"
(196:0.10). His taught philosophy of prayer (144:2–4) distinguishes prayer from worship:
"Prayer is self-reminding — sublime thinking; worship is self-forgetting —
superthinking" (143:7.7); "Prayer is spiritually sustaining, but worship is
divinely creative" (143:7.5);
prayer must be honest, unselfish, trusting, and answered ultimately by capacity
enlargement, not circumstance manipulation (144:4.7–10).

**Decision-making.** The paradigm is Paper 136: the forty days were "not a
period of great temptation but rather the period of the Master's *great
decisions*" (136:4.10) — six policy decisions made "in lone communion with
himself and his Father's immediate presence": (1) subordinate timing to the
Father's unfolding plan (136:4); (2) exclude superhuman intervention — "no
miracle... unless the Father in heaven specifically ruled otherwise" (136:5.5);
(3) submit to natural law in personal need — refusing bread-from-stones
selfishness (136:6.2, 136:6.10); (4) refuse presumption — no pinnacle-leap
spectacle "in defiance of his Father's laws" (136:7.2); (5) refuse compromise
with power — "to have power and steadfastly refuse to use it" (136:8.6); (6)
refuse the dazzling Messianic display for a purely spiritual kingdom (136:9.2);
sealed by the total pledge: "in all other matters... I will be subject to the
will of my Father" (136:10.1). Decisions in ministry followed the same shape:
all-night prayer before choosing the twelve (Gospel parallel Luke 6:12);
withdrawal before crises; Gethsemane as final decision-prayer (182:3).

## Q4. What did "doing the Father's will" mean in his actual life?

"Jesus brought to God, as a man of the realm, the greatest of all offerings:
the consecration and dedication of his own will to the majestic service of
doing the divine will" (196:0.10). Operationally it meant: (a) interpreting
religion wholly in those terms — "Jesus always and consistently interpreted
religion wholly in terms of the Father's will" (196:0.10); (b) equating the
kingdom with that will — "Your kingdom come; your will be done" as "the very
heart of the prayer which he taught" (196:0.8); (c) the *positive* mode of
submission: not passive resignation but active consecration — a life "of
religious devotion to the doing of his Father's will and of consecration to
the unselfish service of man" (196:1.2); (d) under agony, alignment rather
than exemption: Gethsemane's "not my will, but yours, be done" (182:3, with
Gospel parallels Mark 14:36 par.); (e) service as the will's daily content —
"Jesus lived a religion of *service*" (5:4.7). The whole is summarized in the
book's definition of his life-purpose: "doing the Father's will, living the
human life religiously and by faith" (196:0.14).

## Q5. Human faith, divine knowledge, direct vision — or combination?

This is where the classical problem (Aquinas: Christ had vision, therefore not
faith — see `10_HISTORICAL_THEOLOGY.md`) meets its most detailed alternative
account.

**Urantia position (URANTIA REVELATORY CLAIM):** a genuine developmental
combination. The human Jesus exercised real human faith — "the faith of his
mortal intellect" — while ascending "from the nature of man to the
consciousness of the nature of God... by the conjoint achievement of the faith
of his mortal intellect and the acts of his indwelling Adjuster" (196:1.6),
through seven marked stages of "faith consciousness of progressive
divinization" (196:1.6–13). The ascent "was an exclusively mortal achievement"
(196:2.2). His self-limitation was chosen: the great decisions (136:4–10)
excluded superhuman knowledge and power from his personal conduct, so that his
faith operated under authentically human conditions — which is precisely what
makes it shareable: "he ascended from the lowly levels of humanity to become
one with divinity, thus becoming the new and living way whereby all mortals may
so ascend" (196:2.4). Progressive God-consciousness culminated in "the positive
realization of his divine nature" (196:2.2) — vision grew *out of* faith
rather than replacing it from the start.

**Gospel data consistent with development (TEXTUAL OBSERVATION):** "Jesus
increased in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men" (Luke 2:52);
"he learned obedience by the things which he suffered" (Heb 5:8); ignorance of
the day and hour (Mark 13:32).

**INFERENCE:** Any account granting Jesus uninterrupted beatific vision from
conception cannot make him an *exemplar of faith* without equivocation; any
account making him merely a struggling believer cannot carry the Gospels'
authority-claims. Both the subjective-genitive scholars and the Urantia Papers
resolve this by a developmental model in which real human faith is the organ of
a growing divine self-realization. The Urantia account is the more explicit and
systematized version of that move.
